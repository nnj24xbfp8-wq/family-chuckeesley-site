**Rielingshausen** — a small farming village about seven miles northeast of Marbach am Neckar, in Baden-Württemberg — is, in the words of the local historian **Heinz Erich Walter**, *"the cradle of the Wildermuth-Clan."* Two documents in the family papers open up what that means: Walter's essay on the clan's famous members, and Robert Earl Wildermuth's own account of walking the village's church and cemetery in the early 1990s.

Chuck descends specifically from **[Johann Michael Wildermuth](/family/johann-michael-wildermuth/)** of neighboring **Großaspach**, who emigrated to Marietta, Ohio in 1847. He and these famous Wildermuths share the deep Rielingshausen-district stock, but no proven line connects them. What follows is the story of the *name* and its village, not of Chuck's direct pedigree.

## Part I — The famous Wildermuths (after Heinz Erich Walter)

**Ottilie Wildermuth (1817–1877)** is the best-known bearer of the name — a writer, raised in Marbach, whose main street the town later named *Wildermuthstraße*. Per Walter, her own link to Rielingshausen was distant: she came into the clan by marriage. In 1843 she married the university professor **Johann David Wildermuth** (1807–1885) of Pleidelsheim, who had climbed from a poor family to a teaching post and the university at Tübingen, where the couple settled and Ottilie's writing flourished. The Pleidelsheim Wildermuths traced back to **Johann Peter Wildermuth**, born 1682 in Rielingshausen, who married in Pleidelsheim and died of smallpox in 1721 — Ottilie's husband was his great-grandson.

Their son was the psychiatrist **Dr. Hermann Wildermuth (1852–1907)**, director of the Stetten asylum in the Rems valley and, at the last, head of the Board of Health in Stuttgart. Hermann's sons carried the name further still: one, **Hans**, was himself a noted psychiatrist; the other, **Eberhard Wildermuth (1890–1952)**, became the most publicly prominent of all. A lawyer and a decorated major in the First World War, Eberhard was in the Second World War the **commander of Le Havre**, remembered (per Walter) for conducting the city's defense *"with an acknowledged level of decency."* After the Reich's collapse he entered democratic politics, served as economics secretary for Württemberg-Hohenzollern, and in 1949 joined **Chancellor Adenauer's first cabinet as the Federal Republic's first minister for housing**. He died in Tübingen in 1952. Walter records a telling anecdote: asked under the Third Reich to furnish an "Aryan" family tree, Eberhard pointed to the illustrious names on his *mother's* side — unaware of his own Wildermuth descent. His biographer Kohlhaas noted drily that the name Wildermuth — literally "wild spirit/courage" — *"had originally certainly not been given to a pussyfooter."*

Walter even traces the name into Swiss literature: the novelist **Max Frisch** (1911–1991), author of *Homo Faber* and *The Fire Raisers*, recorded in his diary that his maternal grandfather was a Wildermuth whose father *"came from Württemberg"* — a craftsman who had left **Rielingshausen or Pleidelsheim** for Switzerland around 1850, the same mid-century emigration decade in which Johann Michael left for Ohio.

### The Wildermuth mayors — and a murder on the road

The clan ran the village for over a century. Walter lists the Wildermuth mayors (*Schultheißen*) of Rielingshausen: **Zacharias** (1640–1672), **Jakob** (1721–1740), and **Adam** (1740–1762). Adam left office alive but met a grim end. On the night of **23 May 1768**, the retired ex-mayor was found dead on the road between Oppenweiler and Strümpfelbach — robbed and murdered by one of the criminal bands then roaming the Mainhardter Forest, after he had carelessly shown off a purse of 100 Taler at a guest house. The band of some forty was not broken up until 1772, when nineteen were beheaded and the rest sentenced to hard labor.

## Part II — Robert Earl's return to Rielingshausen

Two centuries and an ocean away, **[Robert Earl Wildermuth](/family/robert-earl-wildermuth/)** — Chuck's grandfather — went looking for the village himself. His travelogue records the search. He found **Marbach** first (Schiller's town, and Ottilie's, with its *Wildermuthstraße* and its thick-walled hilltop old town), then, *"after three trips,"* the village itself: **Rielingshausen**, *"the town where the surname Wildermuth is said to have originated,"* with **nineteen Wildermuths in the local telephone directory** and Großaspach — his own great-grandfather's emigration village — twenty miles east.

At the village he found the **old Evangelical church, built in 1752**, its interior *"immaculately clean… the old wooden pews shone as if they were newly lacquered,"* with a high, narrow pulpit *"raised up about eight feet."* He wanted photographs but was so watched by a suspicious cleaning woman that, in his own words, *"I was intimidated… I took no pictures inside the church."*

Then the cemetery. Carved into the sandstone wall by the gate were the names of the village's war dead — *"among those names… were listed twenty-three Wildermuths."* Inside, tombstone after tombstone carried the name. Two elderly women tending a grave stopped to talk, and Robert Earl tried out his broken German:

> *"Meine Name Wildermuth ist. Ich bin ein Amerikaner. My Grossvater nach Amerika aus Grossaspach im 1847."*
>
> (*My name is Wildermuth. I am an American. My grandfather [went] to America from Großaspach in 1847.*)

The women *"started speaking rapidly in German and directed me to… the Wildermuth section of the grave-yard. It was very eerie,"* he wrote, *"to see so many tombstones with the name Wildermuth on them"* — the graves kept in the German manner, each plot *"carefully covered with freshly cut evergreen boughs."* The [war-memorial wall he described](/archive/rielingshausen-war-memorial-wildermuth/) survives in the archive in Chuck's own 2018 photographs — the same *"twenty-three Wildermuths"* Robert Earl counted, the family that stayed.
