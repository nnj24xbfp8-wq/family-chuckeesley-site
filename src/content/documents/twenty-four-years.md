## 二十四年

1966年夏，[周兆帧](/family/zhaozheng-zhou/)从青岛医学院毕业，成绩在班上数一数二，留校的名单上有他的名字。

留下来，是那时最好的去处。留校意味着学校把你要下了：教学医院、城里的工作、一条走得通的路。对他家而言，还不止如此。他父亲[周茂礼](/family/maoli-zhou/)在他一岁多时去世。母亲[庞焕彩](/family/huancai-pang/)独自拉扯三个孩子，自己没有工作，常去菜市场捡人家不要的菜叶，拎回家煮了吃。哥哥[周兆祥](/family/zhaoxiang-zhou/)和姐姐[周秀珍](/family/xiuzhen-zhou/)早早去当学徒工，好让家里有进项。三个孩子里，只有最小的那个一直念了下去。二十八年的这个安排，刚刚供出一个医生，而这个医生本来是要留在青岛的。

然后文革来了，留校名单作废。全国毕业分配停摆，他悬了整整一年：有资格，没去处。学校后来补发了大约八个月的工资，那大概是这套制度所能给出的、最接近一句道歉的东西。

分配终于下来，去处是大西北**宁夏的一所乡镇医院**。仍是医生，仍是国家干部编制 &mdash; 他们这一届虽然延后一年、分去的都是边远地区的乡镇医院，身份并未降等。降的是距离：从青岛到宁夏，两千公里。

他换了。那年学校管理混乱，去艰苦地方的乡镇医院，两个同学彼此同意就算数 &mdash; 不必申请，不必批准。他找到一个愿意换的同学，各自接过对方的去处。同学去了宁夏。他去了山东最北边、黄河以北的阳信县，翟王乡镇医院，全省最穷的地区之一。1967年秋，他到了。二十九岁。

他在那里待了二十四年。

他在阳信成了家。妻子[Su Ying Xian](/family/su-ying-xian/)也是毕业分配到阳信县中医院的医生 &mdash; 同一套分配制度把两个青岛之外的年轻人放到了同一个偏远县城，**他们是在阳信认识的**。双胞胎女儿[周燕](/family/yan-zhou/)、[周伟](/family/wei-zhou/)生于1970年。1974年，他从乡镇医院调进妻子所在的阳信县中医院，第三个女儿[周亮](/family/liang-zhou/)1976年出生。他一级一级往上走，走到副主任医师就停住了，因为一所贫困县医院能给人的位置，到此为止。多年后侄子[周玲](/family/ling-zhou/)说得很直白：换在青岛这样的城市，他早该是主任医师了，卡的是名额。他的职业生涯有个顶，而这个顶是一个地方，不是他这个人。

青岛那边，母亲一天天老下去，儿女都在身边，只少了最小的那个。她整天念叨。她哭到眼睛看不见了。她说她惟一的心事，就是想让他回青岛。

他试过。他托人办调动，办了好多年，侄子周玲还陪着他，提着东西一家一家往经办人家里去。一直没办成。那些年里，人是不能自己搬进城的：城镇户口是配给的，工作由单位分配，从县医院调进青岛，两头都要签字，还得占上一个本来就几乎不存在的名额。

最后办成这件事的是周玲。靠的不是申请，是人。他一个一个地谈，谈了好几年 &mdash; 就像三十年后他女儿看着他在啤酒博物馆里张罗事情的那个样子 &mdash; 终于找到了那个办得成的人。

**1967年秋至1991年**，在阳信二十四年。1991年，调令下来了。五口人一起回青岛：兆帧、妻子，和三个女儿 &mdash; 那年她们二十岁、二十岁、十五岁。夫妇二人都进了青岛市南区浮山所医院。这一纸调令带回城里的是两份医务工作，也把三个年轻姑娘放进了青岛的学校、青岛的日子。丽婕转述父亲的话：她们回来很高兴，日子好了，书也读得好了。

他五十三岁。走的时候二十九岁。2001年退休。

母亲庞焕彩于1992年4月6日去世，在全家迁回约一年之后。她等了大半辈子，等到了，还等到了一年。

那三个女儿中的两位，前不久还与Chuck和丽婕在青岛同席吃饭 &mdash; 这一节，还在继续。

周玲手书这段往事时，在年数那里空了一格，因为他不知道是多少年。

二十四年。

## Twenty-four years

In the summer of 1966, **[Zhou Zhaozheng](/family/zhaozheng-zhou/)** graduated near the top of his class at Qingdao Medical College, and his name was on the list to stay.

Staying was the prize. A retained post meant the college kept you on: a teaching hospital, a city job, a career with somewhere to go. For his family it would have meant something more particular. His father, [Zhou Maoli](/family/maoli-zhou/), had died when he was a year old. His mother, **[Pang Huancai](/family/huancai-pang/)**, raised three children with no work of her own, walking to the vegetable market to gather the leaves other people threw away and carrying them home to cook. His older brother [Zhaoxiang](/family/zhaoxiang-zhou/) and his sister [Xiuzhen](/family/xiuzhen-zhou/) were sent out as apprentice workers so that money would come in. Of the three of them, only the baby was allowed to keep studying. Twenty-eight years of that arrangement had just produced a doctor, and the doctor was going to stay in Qingdao.

Then the Cultural Revolution arrived, and the retained-post list was scrapped. Graduate placement stopped across the country, and he spent a year suspended &mdash; qualified, assigned nowhere. When the college finally settled up it paid him about eight months of back wages, which is the closest thing to an apology the arrangement ever offered.

His posting, when it finally came, was a **township hospital in Ningxia**, in the far northwest. He would still be a doctor, still on the state cadre payroll; their whole delayed cohort went out to township hospitals in remote places without losing that standing. What he lost was proximity. Qingdao to Ningxia is two thousand kilometres.

So he traded. Administration at the college was in disarray that year, and for hardship postings to rural township hospitals it was enough for two classmates to agree between themselves &mdash; no application, no approval. He found someone willing, and they took each other's assignments. The classmate went to Ningxia. Zhaozheng went to the northernmost corner of Shandong, past the Yellow River &mdash; the township hospital at Zhaiwang, in Yangxin County, in one of the poorest prefectures in the province. He arrived in the autumn of **1967**. He was twenty-nine.

He stayed for twenty-four years.

He married there. His wife, **[Su Ying Xian](/family/su-ying-xian/)**, had been assigned to the county traditional-medicine hospital on her own graduation — the same placement system that had put him in the county — and they met in Yangxin. Twin daughters, **[Zhou Yan](/family/yan-zhou/)** and **[Zhou Wei](/family/wei-zhou/)**, were born in 1970. In 1974 he moved from the township hospital up to the county hospital where she worked, and their third daughter, **[Zhou Liang](/family/liang-zhou/)**, was born in 1976. He rose steadily and then stopped, at deputy chief physician, which is as high as a poor county hospital is permitted to promote anyone. His nephew **[Zhou Ling](/family/ling-zhou/)** put it plainly years later: in a city like Qingdao he would have made full chief physician long before. It was a matter of quotas. His career had a ceiling, and the ceiling was a place, not a limitation of his.

Back in Qingdao his mother was growing old with every one of her children near her except the youngest. She talked about it constantly. She cried about it until her eyes failed and she could no longer see. She said the only thing still on her mind was to have him home.

He tried. For years he petitioned for a transfer, and his nephew went with him to the houses of the officials who handled such things, carrying gifts. Nothing came of it. You could not simply move to a city in those years: urban registration was rationed, jobs were allocated by work units, and a transfer from a county hospital into Qingdao needed signatures at both ends and a place in a quota that barely existed.

What finally worked was Zhou Ling. Not a petition &mdash; people. He talked to one person and then another and then another, for years, the same way his daughter watched him work a room at the beer museum thirty years later, and eventually he found the person who could do it.

In **1991** the transfer came through. Five people came home: Zhaozheng, his wife, and their three daughters, who were twenty, twenty, and fifteen. Both parents took posts at the Fushansuo Hospital in Shinan District. The move carried two medical careers back into the city, and it put three young women into Qingdao schools and Qingdao lives. [Lijie](/family/lijie-zhou/), translating her father, said they were very happy to come back &mdash; better living, better education.

Zhaozheng was fifty-three. He had left at twenty-nine. He practised another ten years in his own city and retired in 2001.

His mother died on **6 April 1992**, about a year after they arrived. She had waited most of her life and she got the year.

Two of those daughters had lunch with Chuck and Lijie in Qingdao not long ago, which is the part of this that is still going on.

When Zhou Ling wrote the story out by hand, he left a blank where the number of years should have been, because he did not know it.

Twenty-four.
