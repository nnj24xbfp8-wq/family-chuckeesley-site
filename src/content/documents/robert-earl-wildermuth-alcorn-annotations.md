## What this is

Robert Earl Wildermuth flew a full combat tour as a B-24 navigator with the [400th Bombardment Squadron of the 90th Bomb Group](/docs/90th-bomb-group-jolly-rogers-context/). Decades later he acquired **John S. Alcorn's *The Jolly Rogers: History of the 90th Bomb Group During World War II*** (1981) — the group's definitive published history — and read it the way only a man who had been there could: **he annotated almost every page in his own hand**, correcting the official account, marking the missions he flew, and naming the men he knew.

His marginalia are the rarest kind of source in this archive: a participant's private commentary written directly onto the printed record of his own war. What follows transcribes his handwritten notes, page by page, with only enough of Alcorn's printed context (paraphrased) to make each note make sense.

> *A note on sources and copyright: Alcorn's book is an in-copyright 1981 publication; its text is **paraphrased, not reproduced**, below. The quoted passages are Robert Earl's own handwriting — family-owned — signed **"REW"** or **"RE."** Bracketed words are uncertain readings of the handwriting.*

## December 1944 — the first missions from Biak

Beside Alcorn's account of the group's third Pacific Thanksgiving (23 November 1944), which the official history calls the most elegant feast the Jolly Rogers ever had, Robert Earl set the record straight from the enlisted-and-junior-officer end of the chow line:

> *"Ate my first Thanksgiving dinner sitting in the sand and eating from a mess kit. Mess hall too small. — REW"*

At the foot of the same page, against the text describing the 90th still flying "milk runs" to the Davao area at the close of 1944, he logged his **first combat mission**:

> *"Flew first mission through a horrible thunderstorm about 300 miles out from Biak (20 Dec 1944). Lost an engine after leaving target area and had to fly back on 3 engines. Threw everything overboard. — RE"*

On the facing page, beside the photograph of group commander Lt. Col. Wilson H. Banks, he logged his **second**:

> *"Flew 2nd mission on 25 December 1944 (Christmas Day). Again went to Davao. Christmas dinner enroute to target (cold 'bully beef'). — REW"*

These two entries date the start of his combat flying more precisely than his own [combat log](/docs/the-big-one/) does — a 20 December 1944 first mission and a Christmas Day second, both long overwater runs to Davao from Biak, the second eaten as cold canned beef over the target run. (A separate marginal note near the Banks caption reads **"Wm. Haggert"** — apparently identifying a man in the photograph; its exact reference is uncertain.)

## The Formosa map — Kiirun and Taichu

On Alcorn's map of Formosa (Taiwan) opening the "Formosan Fury" chapter, Robert Earl highlighted the targets he struck and annotated two of them. Beside **Kiirun (Keelung)** in the north:

> *"Very dangerous target. Anti-aircraft manufacturing plant located here. Said to be 365 heavy anti-aircraft gunnery sites located around this target. Went there 3 times. — REW"*

And beside **Taichu (Taichung)**, the firsthand version of the group-lead disaster he also tells in his combat log:

> *"Led group to Taichu. Our first group lead. Bombardier late in setting up bomb sight. As a consequence we led the group over a big naval base. Every plane in group hit by flak except ours. Last time we got to lead. Cost us all a promotion. — REW"*

The Taichu note matches the **3 June 1945** entry in [*The Big One*](/docs/the-big-one/) — the flak-fearing bombardier, the botched turning point, every aircraft in the group hit but his — confirming the story against the map of the target itself.

## Mindoro — on R&R when the group moved

Beside Alcorn's account of the 90th staging up to **Mindoro on 10 January 1945**, Robert Earl noted that he wasn't there for the move:

> *"On R&R leave when group moved up to Mindoro — much nicer in Sydney, Australia anyway. — REW"*

This is the [Sydney R&R](/docs/the-big-one/#sydney-rr) that runs through his combat log — the ten-day leave to the group's leased hotel on King's Cross that stretched to twenty-one days.

## Peleliu — the H2X detachment

Where Alcorn describes the radar-equipped (H2X) Liberators of the 320th and 321st Squadrons detached to the 22nd Bomb Group at Anguar in the Palau Islands, Robert Earl marked his own part in it:

> *"Our plane was H2X equipped so [I] went along to Peleliu Island in the Palau group. Flew 2 night missions from Peleliu. — REW"*

This is the [Peleliu attachment](/docs/the-big-one/) phase of his tour — the night radar missions over Taiwan flown on temporary duty with the 22nd BG. A companion note about the difficulty of the work is only partly legible: *"Our crews [were] unaccustomed [to] night flying …"* — the rest of the passage is not confidently readable.

## 17/18 January 1945 — Jay Strozier

The most striking annotation sits beside Alcorn's account of a **320th Squadron aircraft lost on the night of 17/18 January 1945**, which the book notes Wiley Woods remembered as piloted by a man named **Courtney**. Robert Earl's hand fills the margin:

> *"My best friend and cadet classmate 2 Lt. Jay Strozier was the navigator on this crew. I waited and waited on the morning of 18 January to go to [breakfast] with Jay when he returned. — REW"*

This is the firsthand ground truth behind the [Strosier–Courtney marquee moment](/docs/the-big-one/#strosier-crash) in his combat log — the Selman Field navigation-cadet classmate who had told Robert Earl many times that he never expected to make it home, lost with Lt. Courtney's crew in a thunderstorm off Samar. The combat log spells the name **"Strosier"**; here in Alcorn's margin he writes **"Strozier."** Both are in Robert Earl's own hand; the archive keeps both spellings on the record. The detail his annotation adds — that he *waited and waited that morning of the 18th to go to breakfast with Jay* — is the small, unbearable human fact the official history has no room for.
