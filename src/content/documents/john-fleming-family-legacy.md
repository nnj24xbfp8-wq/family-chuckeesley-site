### JOHN FLEMING FAMILY LEGACY — *6th Great-grandfather*

Family tradition and the research of several Fleming family genealogists has it that the patriarch of the John Fleming line in America came originally to Cecil County, Maryland in 1712. He came from either Scotland or Ireland but he was of Scottish origin. John's wife's name was Jean but at this time her maiden name is unknown. When and where they were married is also unknown (something for some future family genealogist to discover). In 1714, they gave birth to their first son, John Jr. (the junior part is of my choosing for the sake of keeping all of the Johns straight). They were soon joined by sons and brothers James in 1716 and Samuel in 1718. (I have some reservations about the son named Samuel because John Sr. never mentioned him in his Will).

How long John Sr. stayed in Cecil County is unknown but in the years 1754, 1755 and 1756 he was listed on the tax rolls of Derry Township, Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. (This part of Lancaster County is now Dauphin County). It is easy to understand John's travel to Lancaster County by looking at the map. The Susquehanna River flows down from Pennsylvania and empties into Chessapeake Bay right at Cecil County and waterways were a main highway of travel.

Family tradition had John Sr.'s place of death as Derry, Pennsylvania. I had much research to do to locate Derry for I was looking for a town named Derry instead of a township. With the aid of a very helpful archivist in Chester County, Pennsylvania, I was able to find a copy of John's Will by using his known death date. John's will shows him to have been very successful in life for he left his children considerable amounts of money for that time period. The money was in English pounds sterling. John was a weaver by profession. Scottish woven fabrics were much wanted throughout Europe and that is one reason for a large emigration of Scots. The King of England had placed an embargo on Scottish woolens that demanded that all woven materials be sent to London where a pitifully small price was paid.

John and Jean Fleming had the following children:

- John born in 1714. Married Anne White.
- James born in 1716. Married Barbara Devilbliss
- Samuel (?) born in 1718. Married Allice Charlton.
- Margaret born (?). Married Thomas Ramsey
- Mary born (?). Married George Murry
- Elenor born (?). Married James Patton
- Ann born (?). Unmarried at the time of her father's Will.

John Fleming Senior died sometime in May 1777 for his Will was probated on 17 May 1777.

### JOHN FLEMING JR.

John Fleming Jr. (My designation). This John Fleming, son of John Fleming Sr. and Jean ( ? ) born in Cecil County, Maryland in 1714 shortly after his father came to America. John lived in Frederick County, Maryland (now Montgomery County) his entire life. He had a large plantation as well as some smaller farms located near a trading post known as Cabin John. His plantation was near what is now Washington, D.C. but there was no Washington D.C. then. He was a prosperous tobacco grower. Tobacco was such an important crop that it actually was used as money on the common market. John Jr. married Anne White, daughter of Robert and Ann (Burgess) White.

On 5 February 1777, a year after the Revolutionary War started, the province of Maryland passed a law known as the Act Of Assembly and it required all persons engaged in inter and intrastate trade and those desiring the right to vote to take an Oath of Fidelity and Support virtually pledging allegiance and loyalty to the province of Maryland and denouncing loyalty to the King of England. In 1778, John and his sons John III and James took that oath. This made them all sworn patriots and is the basis for all female descendents to become members of the Daughters of the American Revolution.

### JOHN FLEMING III — *4th Great-grandfather*

John Fleming III (My designation) was born circa 1744 in what was then Frederick County, Maryland. He was the son of John and Ann (White) Fleming. On 20 November 1777, he married Nancy Ann Hopkins, daughter of John and Eleanor (Wallace) Hopkins. In 1781, the year that his son John IV was born, John, his father-in-law John Hopkins and several of the Wallaces and Whites moved to the Western frontier in Fallowfield Township, Washington County, Pennsylvania. Quite a move for a one year old son. John III and his father-in-law John Hopkins joined the 5th Battalion, Washington County Militia, commanded by Colonel Thomas Crooks. His company commander was Benjamin White. These men became Indian fighters on the frontier. Fallowfield Township was near Wheeling, West Virginia where there was a strong fort that commanded all river traffic on the Ohio River. The British wanted this fort very badly for it was a thorn in their sides.

### JOHN ROE — *4th Great-grandfather*

John Roe, father-in-law) of John Fleming IV. was born in Morris County, New Jersey circa 1760. In 1779, he enlisted in the Revolutionary Army and stayed for the duration. Fayette County is diagonally across the wide State of Pennsylvania and John and his family of three young children under the age of 8 and a pregnant wife journied that long, tortous route through the forests and over the Allegheny Mountains.

### LEWIS FLEMING — *2nd Great-grandfather*

Lewis Fleming represents a break in the long line of first born males named John. For a long time, I was perplexed by this for the Scottish custom was always to name the first born male John. After finding John Roe's family it's easy to understand how it came about for John and Clarissa named their children after Clarissa's brothers and sisters. Guess who "wore the pants" in that family when it came to naming children.

Not much is known about Lewis Fleming. He left few civil records. He was born circa 1807 on his father's farm on Simpson's Creek in the eastern sector of Harrison County, Virginia (now Taylor County, West Virginia). In 1827, Lewis gained his father's permission to marry Synthia Bailey, daughter of Thomas and Nancy (Bartlett) Bailey. In a note to the "Clearke" of Harrison County, John Fleming wrote the following: **"This is to certify that I, John Fleming have nothing against my son giting married, going from under my house this 14 day 1827 in the year of our Lord. Mr. John Fleming"**. Shortly thereafter, Lewis gained the permission of Synthia's father, Thomas Bailey, Lewis and Synthia were married 14 March 1827.

### JAMES WESLEY FLEMING — *Grandfather*

JAMES WESLEY FLEMING, the son of Thomas and Martha (Turner) Fleming was born in Arnold's Creek in what is now Doddridge County, West, Virginia about five miles west of the town of West Union. He was born on 20 December 1857. On the 7th of October 1885, he married Verona Belle Kelley/Dunbar/Sheppard. They were married in Parkersburg, Wood County, West Virginia. James Fleming was a man born out of his time period. The industrial Revolution was in full swing and all James knew was farming. He was poor and for most of his life he worked as a share-cropper. This was fine for supplying his large family with food but brought no hard cash into the family for everyday family needs. Industry finally caught up to him in about 1910 when he moved his family across the Ohio River into Marietta, Ohio. He went to work for one of Marietta's largest employers, the Marietta Chair Factory. He was given the job of dipping the chairs into a large staining vat. Shortly thereafter, he complained that the fumes from the paint vat was causing him to go blind and he quit his job and went back to sharecropping.

### FLEMING FABLES

**LAVERNA FLEMING RAISON**, Daughter of James and Verona Fleming relates about her life in the log cabin on the Ohio River near St. Mary's, Wood County, West Virginia. She tells that at her birth, her mother told her older brother, Walter, to go "fetch" the midwife. She told him to "bundle up" against the cold March wind. He picked up his cap that was lying on the wood pile next to the fireplace and a snake popped out.

Laverna tells us about the time she lived in Churchtown just west of Marietta. At that time, her mother who was pregnant fell down the "spring house" steps causing a premature birth of baby Grace. Being premature caused Grace to be very frail and she could only thrive on boiled water and "separated milk" (whey). While baby Grace was still quite young, Laverna contracted scarlett fever which swept the close knit family. Baby Grace caught the fever and died.

**CYNTHIA FLEMING**, the daughter of Thomas and Martha Fleming left home at a fairly early age and went to St. Louis, Missouri. She made periodic visits back home and always tried to entice her young nieces to go back to St. Louis with her. None would. On one such visit, she talked her cousin Josephine Fleming's brother into going with her. He lived in St. Louis for about two years when one day at work he fell into a huge vat that he was cleaning and broke both arms and a leg. He ultimately died of these injuries.

Cynthia over the years had grown quite wealthy in the "boarding house" business and it is said that at the time of her death she owned a whole "city block" in St. Louis. Upon her death, she left her property and wealth to the Catholic Church.

**ULYSES GRANT FLEMING**, the son of Thomas and Martha Fleming was indeed a rogue. When his mother died, he refused to go to the cemetery and remained at the family home. While the funeral was underway, Ulyses loaded all of the household furniture into a qagon and rode off never to be heard from again, While on a field trip in 1987 to Atlanta, Georgia and while cleaning up old cemeteries as a project, Susan Raison, daughter of Laverna Fleming Raison came upon a tombstone with the name Ulyses Fleming engraved upon it. It listed the names and addresses of those who had visited the cemetery. I copied several names one of which was Josephines. She gave her address as San Jose, California. I wrote to her and in a few weeks I received a letter from Merritt Island, Florida a town about 50 miles from my home in Casselberry, Florida. It was from Josephine. She was living with her daughter and son-in-law who was in the Space Industry and who had just been transferred from San Jose to Cape Canaveral. I scheduled a visit with Josephine who as it turmed out was 83 years old but looked in her 50's. She had been a schoo; teacher all of her life and her mind was sharp as a 30 year old. With my tape recorder going, we had about a three hour reminiscing session. She said if we were up in West Virginia she could take right to my mother's old cabin. She said she and her young brothers and sisters really liked to go over and visit Aunt Verona. Josephine's father was a strict disciplinarian and run his family much like a Quaker family. "No more talking; no more fun; no more chewing chewing gum etc. At Aunt Veronas all of the kids , though obedient, did whatever they pleased. They went barefooted, took lunches down to the Ohio River bank, built bon fires and were free spirits.

She told how the girls would all climb into an old john boat and how the boys from both families would push them way out in the Ohio River and then with a rope they'd tow them in. On one such time, she and all of her female Fleming cousins, including my mother, put their names and a message telling the finder to get in touch with them. They then threw the bottles in the Ohio River. A short time later a young man came calling. He was from Parkersburg, West Virginia about 25 miles down river from where the bottles were released. He became a suitor to my Aunt Lenora and they were later married.

**LAVERNA FLEMING.** Laverna tells of an epidemic typhoid fever thay swept the James Fleming famiky. One hot summer day Verona Belle and her young daughter May were out in their garden watering and weeding their tomato plants. They were using water from a nearby stream and little May was taking a canful of water for each plant. In the hot sun, May became thirsty and drank from her can. She contracted typhoid fever and died within a few days. The typhoid lived on however and all members pf the family came down sick' It affected Laverna's vocal cords and through out her life she had a husky, raspy voice.

This illustrates what was brought out at a genealogy seminar that I attended. The guest speaker was a genealogist and a medical doctor. He had made a study of the causes of death among our ancestors of yje late 18th and early 19th centuries. When asked what was the greatest caose of death of these sturdy pioneers, his response was "FILTH". No fresh running water, many times no soap and few baths. A farmer would come in from the dusty fields and pull up a chair and eat his dinner without washing.
