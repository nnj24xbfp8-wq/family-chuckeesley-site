## What the letter is

A letter from advanced training in the spring, and one of the most substantial pre-Vietnam letters in the corpus, because it is where the marriage question is actually argued out. On the surface it's weapons training, M-72 rocket launchers fired at a cost he can't help tallying. Underneath it is the real subject: a ring, her parents, his parents, and whether two people can marry with an Army discharge and a war still between them and a life. Charlie's answer is characteristically plain: *"I'm still willing to marry you it is just that things such as discharges will have to work out."*

## Transcription

> Dear Terrie,
>
> I got two letters from you today. The first ones since I've been here. From your letters I believe you are receiving my letters. There have been several days when I haven't written.
>
> Training seems to be reverting back to the same old thing. It should be more interesting which doesn't mean it will be much better. Today we fired M-72 rocket launchers at $47 apiece. We each fired 2. I guess that comes to close to $20,000. Nice weapon.
>
> I'm sorry your vacation comes around Easter. I should be able to see you then, and unless I'm forced to stay here. As far as a ring, then, I don't know. I realize that I should just get one but I want to get the best for my $20.00.
>
> What is your parents' reaction to us? You asked about what my parents would think if we got married before you finished school. I think it is primarily what happened to Jeanne and that they don't want to support us for [page 2:] a while. Also I doubt if things will work out that soon. That would be the case only if everything worked out. Don't plan anything.
>
> Lights are out so I'm going to have to end. I'm still willing to marry you it is just that things such as discharges will have to work out. Yes I will probably go back with Sears unless something big comes up. I doubt if I do much looking. Be a good girl and don't get too horny.
>
> Love
> Charles

## What the letter records

**The M-72 and the $20 ring.** *"Today we fired M-72 rocket launchers at $47 apiece... close to $20,000. Nice weapon."* The Army's careless expense, thousands of dollars fired off in an afternoon, sits directly against the *"$20.00"* Charlie is trying to stretch into an engagement ring. He doesn't draw the contrast himself, but the two numbers a paragraph apart make it: the war can spend twenty thousand dollars on a training exercise; a private planning a marriage has twenty.

**The marriage argument.** This is the fullest the marriage question gets in the training letters. Terrie had asked what his parents would think about marrying before she finished school, and Charlie answers honestly: the worry is *"primarily what happened to Jeanne"* &mdash; his sister &mdash; *"and that they don't want to support us for a while."* He is careful with her hopes (*"Don't plan anything"*), but the bottom line is unequivocal: *"I'm still willing to marry you it is just that things such as discharges will have to work out."* The tour and the discharge are the obstacles, not the intention.

**"Go back with Sears."** *"I will probably go back with Sears unless something big comes up."* The civilian job waiting on the far side, the Sears path the [April 1969 spring-break letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1969-04-florida-spring-break/) had recorded him interviewing for in his Marietta College senior year, before the draft intervened. Here it's the default future he'd return to after the Army.

**The teasing close.** *"Be a good girl and don't get too horny."* The dry, affectionate sign-off, the same register as the [Christmas 1970 letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-12-25-christmas-bob-hope/)'s homesick candor, carrying the seriousness of the marriage talk out on a joke.
