## What the letter is

1 July 1970 — one of the earliest surviving letters of the Vietnam tour, written as Charlie's first full month in-country closes. *"The month is over with, I have survived and we still have made little contact. Only that once."* The tone is still finding its register: relief at having gotten through the first stretch, practical logistics (packages, letters, ammunition loads), and an early, generous answer to the marriage question — *wherever she wants, he'll go for it* — before the letter turns back to the field stretch that keeps running longer than planned.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**Fifty days in, and the world Charlie left**

Charlie's *"I've been here 50 days now"* places his arrival in-country around mid-May 1970 — squarely inside the most convulsive stretch of the war's home front. **Kent State** (4 May 1970) and the killing of four student protesters had touched off nationwide campus strikes just as he was landing in Vietnam; **Nixon's Cambodian incursion**, launched 30 April 1970 and withdrawn by the end of June per his own deadline, was the operation those protests were reacting against. Charlie's letter — written the day after that withdrawal deadline — describes a war of small-unit patrols and enemy complexes spotted from helicopters, entirely disconnected from the upheaval happening on the campuses Terrie was attending. The two timelines run through this corpus side by side without ever quite touching.

*Sources: [Wikipedia: Kent State shootings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kent_State_shootings); [Wikipedia: Cambodian campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodian_campaign).*

## Transcription

> *1 July 70*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> Well the month is over with, I have survived and we still have made little contact. Only that once. After the[next] resupply we will be carrying more ammo which will make things harder, but I guess safer, esp[ecially] at night.
>
> You asked if you could send packages. Of course, but they are expensive to send and I must ask that you keep them small as it is hard enough to carry stuff as it is. Also, somehow coordinate with home before you send something so two packages aren't hard to carry at once. Just keep sending letters letting me know what and how you are doing. I keep your letters for
>
> [page 2:] several days and try to answer your questions, but if I don't, just keep asking them. I have to burn them after a while.
>
> Well at the most I have less than 350 days left and at the least I have 3[0]5 or so. I hope time keeps going fast. I've been here 50 days now. It just doesn't seem that long, and then it seems a lifetime.
>
> What are you taking in summer school? How many hours do you have and how many more do you have before you graduate?
>
> Wher[ever] you want to get married? It's up to you, you know. Most anything you want I'll go for.
>
> It seems we will be out in the field longer than I thought. When
>
> [page 3:] we come in the 5th or so it will only be 18 hrs or so. I don't think I'll ever be clean. I don't really smell that bad because I wash off with water every day. Maybe I'll get in the habit.
>
> It seems we will be with the third platoon for a while. The helicopters have spotted an enemy complex and we will be heading that way. Oh well, maybe my luck of no contact will hold. I hope so.
>
> I can't really think of much to say except keep writing and praying for me. I love you very much and I'm waiting for you. Til next resupply date.
>
> Love
> Charles

## What the letter records

**"The month is over with, I have survived."** The plainest possible statement of the tour's actual unit of measurement — not days exactly, but survived months, stacked one on another. It is the same accounting that later letters make explicit with day counts (*"144 days,"* *"227 days,"* *"305 days"*); here, a month in, the count is still fresh enough to be marked as a small victory in itself.

**Burning the letters.** *"I keep your letters for several days... I have to burn them after a while."* A detail rarely stated so directly — in the field, paper was weight and a security liability, and burning read mail was standard practice. It quietly explains why Terrie's side of the correspondence doesn't survive.

**"Wherever you want to get married? ... Most anything you want I'll go for."** An early, generous version of a decision he'll spend the next year returning to with more constraints attached — money, timing, his own preferences about apartments and honeymoons. It's worth reading against the [Aug 5 letter's](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-08-05-ripcord-and-casualties/) worry about marrying into debt, and the far more detailed wedding-logistics letters of [September](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-09-07-firebases-and-the-honeymoon/) and beyond — the same question, answered with steadily less blank-check freedom as the actual wedding gets closer.

**The enemy complex.** *"The helicopters have spotted an enemy complex and we will be heading that way."* One of the earliest field-operations details in the surviving corpus, stated with the same flat calm that runs through every combat mention here — followed immediately by *"maybe my luck of no contact will hold."* Fifty days in, the luck was still holding.
