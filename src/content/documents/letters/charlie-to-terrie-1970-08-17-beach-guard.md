## What the letter is

17 August 1970 &mdash; one of the very few summer letters that survive, sitting in the gap between the [July 1970 letters](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-07-18-no-mail/) and the [September malaria letters](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-09-21-malaria-hospital/). Charlie writes on a Wednesday morning after twelve hours of beach-guard duty as a lifeguard, and he has been drinking. The occasion for the letter is other men's marriages coming apart: a friend who hasn't heard from his wife in five months, and another soldier who broke down and cried, wanting home because his wife was leaving him. Against that, Charlie writes Terrie the only thing the night seems to require &mdash; that there is no comparison, that she is the one.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**A faint letter from the summer gap**

This is a hard letter to read &mdash; a pencil original, lightly written, that has faded. The transcription below is a best-effort reading; illegible passages are marked, and a cleaner copy survives in the family's grandmaE1.pdf master scan against which it can later be verified and completed.

What it records is clear enough in outline. By August 1970 Charlie had been in country a couple of months, and the *"dear John"* letter &mdash; the wife or girlfriend back home who stops writing, or writes to end it &mdash; was the ambient dread of every married or attached man in the unit. The drinking, the crying soldier, the friend with five months of silence: this is the home-front anxiety of the war seen from the inside, and Charlie's response is to reassure the woman he intends to marry that he is not one of those stories. The beach-guard lifeguard duty places him at a rear or coastal stand-down rather than in the field.

</aside>

## Transcription

> *Aug 17*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> [I] got your package [&hellip;] yesterday. It is Wednesday morning and I have just pulled 12 hours of beach guard as a lifeguard [&hellip;] to look at the children. I'm [drunk &hellip;] you didn't know [&hellip;] but before I sleep tonight I feel there are a few things you should know. My dear Terrie [&hellip;]
>
> [&hellip;] is beautiful. As yet I haven't [&hellip;] and I don't think I will. I got drunk with a friend [&hellip;] over here [who] hasn't heard from his wife in 5 months. Also a guy broke down and cried &mdash; he wants [to go] home because his wife was going out on him. (Hope you can read this.) Now there is no comparison with you and the other women. But if you ask when we will be married &mdash; ???
>
> [&hellip; remainder illegible &hellip;]

## What the letter records

**"Hope you can read this."** Charlie's own aside, written in a pencil he clearly knew was hard to make out &mdash; a line that has only become truer with fifty years of fading. It is preserved here exactly because it is his.

**The dear-John ambient dread.** The friend with five months of silence and the soldier crying over a wife who left are the same fear that runs under the whole correspondence &mdash; the reason Charlie reads every mail gap as a verdict, and the reason a drunken late-night letter resolves into a reassurance that *there is no comparison.*

**The summer gap.** The corpus thins between July and September 1970; this letter is one of the few markers of those months, and the beach-guard detail is a rare glimpse of a rear-area lull before the September malaria hospitalization.
