## What the letter is

Christmas morning, 25 December 1970. Charlie is on guard, the night hot and muggy, and the holiday brings *"really don't feel much cheer."* The bright spot is the Bob Hope show, which he files with his usual dryness &mdash; like the TV version, most of the jokes flat, but the girls a welcome change, and worth it just to say he was there. The mail brings strange Christmas cards from strangers (his APO address had run in a newspaper) along with notes from Terrie's family. Sober, homesick, and missing her mail, he signs off promising to write more often.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**The Bob Hope Christmas show, 1970**

Bob Hope's USO Christmas tour played Vietnam every December of the war, and the 1970 tour was one of the last of the big in-country shows. Charlie's verdict here &mdash; *"most of his jokes fell flat but the girls weren't [bad]... at least now I'll be able to say I've seen Bob Hope"* &mdash; is the grunt's-eye view of a spectacle pitched as much at the cameras and the home front as at the troops. He repeats the shrug three weeks later in the [13 January letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1971-01-13-rats-and-bob-hope/): *"really wasn't worth much except to say you've been there."*

The *"weird X-mas cards"* came because servicemen's APO addresses were published in hometown papers and circulated by write-a-soldier campaigns &mdash; a small, well-meant civilian ritual that filled Charlie's Christmas mail with notes from people he'd never met.

*Source: [Wikipedia: Bob Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Hope) (USO Christmas tours of Vietnam).*

</aside>

## Transcription

> *Dec 25*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> Well here it is Christmas morning and I really don't feel much cheer. It is a hot muggy night and I'm on guard once again.
>
> I did see the Bob Hope Show and it is just like the TV show. Most of his jokes fell flat but the girls weren't [bad] &mdash; it was nice for a change. I should have slept but at least now I'll be able to say I've seen Bob Hope.
>
> I'm getting all sorts of weird X-mas cards since my address apparently was in the Times. I didn't know there were so many daffy people in the world. I also got a note from your parents and Sandy too. Well Sandy &mdash; I love her and want her body! I'm sober but terribly horny.
>
> Well &mdash; merry X-mas. I'm trying to be a good boy. I'll probably get drunk tomorrow. I haven't received much mail from you lately. Well as of today I still love you. I'll try to write at least every other day.
>
> Love Charles

## What the letter records

**Christmas without cheer.** The flattest holiday note in the corpus &mdash; hot, muggy, on guard, homesick. Charlie doesn't dress it up; the cheer is supplied, weakly, by Bob Hope and the novelty of strangers' cards.

**The teasing about Sandy.** *"Well Sandy &mdash; I love her and want her body!"* &mdash; the kind of in-family ribbing (Sandy being Terrie's sister, [Sandra](/family/sandra-sue-wildermuth-clement/)) that runs through the letters; affectionate, a little outrageous, and aimed at making Terrie laugh from eight thousand miles away.

**"Trying to be a good boy."** The refrain of the whole correspondence &mdash; the fidelity he keeps insisting on, here paired honestly with *"terribly horny"* and the expectation he'll get drunk the next day. The mix of longing, boredom, and resolve is the whole mood of the rear-area Christmas.
