## What the letter is

Around February 1971, from the clerk's desk. The whole letter is Charlie running the arithmetic of getting out: the 150-day early-out rule, a late-June discharge that feels impossibly far off, and a 12-week NCO school dangled in front of him that he'd half like to take &mdash; except it would eat into the early-out he's counting on. He decides to *"play it by ear."* Around the calculation sits the ordinary clerk day (hectic, full of typos, no passes) and a glance at the war, which seems to be heating up faster than the Army is pulling out.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**The 150-day early-out, and the school that would cost it**

The rule Charlie lays out was real Vietnam-era policy: a soldier returning from an overseas tour with **150 days or less left on his service obligation** could be discharged early; with more than 150 days left, he served down to that point. It's the same lever behind the *drops* of the winter letters &mdash; the difference between going home in spring and serving into summer. The catch here is a **12-week NCO school**: it wouldn't lengthen his two-year obligation, but the time in school would push his remaining days back above the 150-day line and cost him the early-out. Hence *"I'm going to have to play it by ear."*

His read on the war &mdash; *"it seems like the war is heating up... they['re not] pulling out fast enough to affect me"* &mdash; fits February 1971, with Operation Lam Son 719 underway and the I Corps drawdown not yet fast enough to shorten his own clock.

*Sources: [Wikipedia: Operation Lam Son 719](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Lam_Son_719); [Wikipedia: Vietnamization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamization).*

</aside>

## Transcription

> Dear Terrie,
>
> From all I've heard I can get out of the service 150 days early if I have 150 days or less after spending a year overseas. If they keep delaying me I may just come out about right. If I have over 150 days they make you serve to 150 days plus whatever over you have. If everything works out I should be able to get out sometime late in June 1971. That is a long way off and is very depressing to think about.
>
> Today was rather hectic at playing clerk. I made a lot of typing mistakes but things should get better. The day passes quickly and I'm not worried about getting fired. I guess we don't get any passes so seeing you is out of the question.
>
> NCO school, although a good [page 2:] thing, is 12 weeks long and although it wouldn't interfere with my 2 yr term (I wouldn't have to serve any longer than 2 yrs) it would cut into my hoped-for 150 day early out. I'm going to have to play it by ear and see what the deal is.
>
> It seems like the war is heating up. It doesn't look like they are pulling out fast enough to affect me. Oh well.
>
> I'm glad to hear you are still in school. Are you going to need any more money? Speak up now. I know it is going to be expensive. Keep writing and be good.
>
> Love Charles

## What the letter records

**The whole tour as arithmetic.** By February 1971 Charlie's days are literally numbered, and every option &mdash; the school, the drop, the extension &mdash; is weighed only by what it does to the count. The 12-week NCO school is *"a good thing"* he turns down in his head because it would cost him 12 weeks of freedom.

**"Playing clerk."** The self-deprecating name for the rear job that kept him out of the field &mdash; hectic, full of typos, but safe, and the day *"passes quickly."* The contrast with the [Thanksgiving field letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-11-25-thanksgiving-field/) three months earlier is the whole arc of his tour.

**"The war is heating up... not pulling out fast enough to affect me."** Charlie reads the Lam Son escalation purely through self-interest: not as a tragedy unfolding, but as bad news for his early-out. It's the short-timer's tunnel vision, honestly recorded.
