## What the letter is

18 March 1971. Three days after the catch-up batch of six letters reached him, Charlie writes back in a noticeably lighter register. **88 days left**, and "a good feeling getting better." He has Terrie's letters of the 8th and 9th, one of which admitted she still hadn't told her parents about the Hawaii plan. Rather than press, Charlie hands her the whole decision: the trip isn't vital, he can cancel at any date, and if her parents or her studies get in the way *he won't be hurt.* The reassurance at the end &mdash; *I still love you and I'm not trying to get rid of you* &mdash; is the giveaway that the low-pressure posture is itself a kind of anxiety, the careful wording of a man who badly wants the answer to be yes.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**"It is up to you": the Hawaii R&R and the home-front negotiation**

The decision Charlie keeps deferring to Terrie was not just a travel plan; it was a near-elopement. Hawaii R&R for a serviceman and his fiancée required the couple to travel under military orders, and the cheapest, cleanest way to do it &mdash; discussed openly across these March letters &mdash; was for Terrie to fly *as Mrs. Eesley*, which meant marrying around the trip. That is why her parents' approval looms so large in the correspondence: the Hawaii question and the marriage question had collapsed into one. Hawaii was the destination of choice for exactly this situation &mdash; in the peak years roughly 11,000 servicemen a month flowed through Honolulu on R&R, a large share of them married or about to be &mdash; and the seven-day allowance (versus five for closer destinations) made it the one realistic window for couples separated by the war.

## Transcription

> *Mar 18*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> Everything is still fine here. I have about 88 days left and it is a good feeling getting better. I got your letters of 8-9 yesterday. In one you mentioned you had not told your parents yet. I hope they approve. It isn't vital that we meet in Hawaii. 88 days and we will be together quite awhile. Everything is set from this end. I can cancel at any date and if your reasons (ie. parents or studies) interfere I won't be hurt. It is up to you so &mdash; let me know when you get everything worked out one way or the other. I still love you and I'm not trying to get rid of you. I'll be waiting for your reply to this letter.
>
> Love Charles

## What the letter records

**"88 days and we will be together quite awhile."** The argument Charlie makes to himself as much as to Terrie: even if Hawaii falls through, the separation is nearly over. The countdown has dropped from the 101 of the 5 March letter, the "drops" steadily shortening the tour.

**"If your reasons (ie. parents or studies) interfere I won't be hurt."** The studied generosity here is doing a lot of work. Terrie was finishing a semester at the University of Maryland, and her parents had not yet been won over to a plan that amounted to marrying in order to make the trip. Charlie's strategy across the spring is to remove every ounce of pressure and let her arrive at yes on her own &mdash; while making sure, in the last line, that she knows what he is hoping for.

**"I'm not trying to get rid of you."** A small, telling reassurance. The low-key tone of the letter could be misread as cooling off; Charlie heads that reading off before it can start.
