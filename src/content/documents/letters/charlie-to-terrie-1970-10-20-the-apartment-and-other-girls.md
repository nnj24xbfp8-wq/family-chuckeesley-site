## What the letter is

20 October 1970, four pages written waiting for a resupply helicopter in the mountains — one of the fullest domestic-planning letters in the corpus, and one of the few that mentions, without any apparent anxiety on his part, that Terrie is not his only correspondent. Charlie reassures her about a friend's behavior, proposes Hawaii as an alternative to worry, tracks a stock investment, manages the family care-package logistics, and raises living together before marriage as simply *"the thing to do."* The letter ends, as many field letters do, on a rumor about when the company might finally leave the mountains.

## Transcription

> *Oct 20*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> Well today in the day we go to the mountain[s] & get resupplied too. It rained last night & this morning but has stopped for now. We are just sitting around waiting for the helicopter.
>
> It sounds like Sandy is really acting crazy. I hope she doesn't act too cheap. Just keep your own self dear. I haven't seen a female since I left to [the] hospital, and they were nurses — nice but not really a type. Don't worry about me. Maybe you should meet me in Hawaii. I could probably arrange to be there for [Jan or] Feb sometime. I've almost given up on bringing anything back except stereo equipment which I'll mail home anyway.
>
> [page 2:] At least the stock market is going back up. My [$300] in G.E. — at that rate it will take me 5 months or so to break even. It will probably take longer than that, but at least it's a start in the right direction.
>
> It is starting to rain again. I got two letters from you yesterday; kind of figured that my aunt & uncle had gone on a trip. Yes, they are delivering packages. I believe I gave you a hint. I am getting goodies from home but I can use as much as I can too. Don't waste your money though. I don't want you to sell anything but goodies for Xmas. Don't expect anything for Xmas & birthday except letters. I'll make it up
>
> [page 3:] to you later, if [it's] too much trouble — I really can't get anything worthwhile [here]. Well, when does school let out in the spring? Also, are you planning to live there or work this summer? I guess I'll probably go back to Sears unless something better comes up. I'd just try another store under a different manager. Do you want to get an apartment with me til we are married? It seems to be the thing to do.
>
> I get letters from a lot of other girls: Sandy, Claudia (yesterday, I'm supposed to write), [the] wife of one (Larry Abbott's sister, who is a senior at OSU whom I've never met). I answer all of them, but you still get the most letters. Well, the rumors are still the
>
> [page 4:] same about us coming back to the lowlands Nov 2. I sure hope so, I'll know more later.
>
> Well, this letter is starting to drag so I guess I'll end.
>
> Love
> Charles

## What the letter records

**Reassurance without much anxiety.** *"I haven't seen a female since I left to the hospital... don't worry about me."* Charlie's answer to whatever concern Terrie raised about Sandy is brief and confident, and he pivots immediately to solving the actual problem — distance — with a concrete plan: Hawaii, February, an R&R booking he could arrange. It's the same instinct for logistics-over-reassurance that runs through the [later actual Hawaii planning](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-11-28-the-hawaii-answer/).

**"Do you want to get an apartment with me til we are married?"** Stated as a practical suggestion rather than a romantic one — *"it seems to be the thing to do"* — consistent with his running worry in the [5 August letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-08-05-ripcord-and-casualties/) about not wanting to marry into debt. Living together first is presented as the financially sensible option, not a departure from a plan so much as an adjustment to one.

**"I get letters from a lot of other girls... you still get the most letters."** The single most striking sentence in the letter is also the one Charlie spends the least time on. Sandy, Claudia, and a stranger's wife are named and dismissed in two sentences, the reassurance built entirely on volume — she gets more mail than any of them — rather than on any accounting of what he writes back. Whether this candor was itself the reassurance, or simply Charlie's habitual bluntness, the letter doesn't say.

**The small economy of a soldier's savings.** A stock investment tracked from the field, care packages routed through an aunt and uncle, an explicit request for letters instead of gifts at Christmas — the same domestic bookkeeping that runs under the whole correspondence, managed from a mountain waiting on a helicopter.
