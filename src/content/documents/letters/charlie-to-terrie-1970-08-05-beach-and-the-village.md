## What the letter is

5 August 1970 — a second, shorter letter written the same day as the [longer Ripcord letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-08-05-ripcord-and-casualties/), from the beach rather than the field. Most of it is ordinary: a Daytona Beach daydream, an admission of falling behind on correspondence, a question about graduation. Then, without much preamble, Charlie reports something he witnessed rather than participated in — a group of men from his unit visiting a nearby village the night before — and tells Terrie plainly that he's naming it because he thought she'd want to know what the war actually looked like around him, not just the parts of it that were dangerous.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**Villages near U.S. bases in Vietnam**

The economic relationship between American military bases and the Vietnamese villages that grew up around them was well documented and often exploitative on both sides: informal markets, black-market goods, and sex work all became part of a local wartime economy shaped by the presence of well-paid foreign troops. Local children acting as intermediaries — for theft, for currency exchange, or as go-betweens for adult transactions — was a widely reported feature of that economy, and one many soldiers' letters and memoirs from the era describe with a mix of wariness and discomfort, much as Charlie does here. It is one of the plainer, less-guarded windows in this corpus into the everyday, non-combat texture of the war around him.

</aside>

## Transcription

> *Aug 5*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> I'm here at the beach now and will be out in the jungle again tomorrow. It was very relaxing. It is hard to say when I'll be back in from the field again, but please just keep on writing.
>
> I can't wait til I get back and can go to Daytona Beach with you. It will probably seem freezing down there for me.
>
> Once again I didn't get anywhere near the letters written that I wanted to. I guess I'll have to wait for a more peaceful time in the jungle.
>
> School should be over for you by the time you receive this letter. How did you do? Do you know yet whether you will graduate next spring?
>
> A lot of guys went down to the village last night to relieve their horniness. Some succeeded, but most were attacked by the children, who will swipe anything of value you have. The kids also pimp for the girls. I didn't go down. I just thought you
>
> [page 2:] might be interested in what goes on over here.
>
> I've been a good boy for the 85 days I've been over here so far.
>
> As things now stand I'll be home June 12, so I have a little over 300 days left over here. I'm counting every day.
>
> We'll have to go now so I'll end the letter now & start another one.

## What the letter records

**A report, not a confession.** *"I didn't go down. I just thought you might be interested in what goes on over here."* Charlie is careful to place himself outside the events he describes, and frames the whole passage as information rather than anecdote — closer in register to his combat reports than to anything intimate. "I've been a good boy for the 85 days" reads as the point of the whole paragraph: an accounting of his own fidelity, using what he'd just described as the visible standard he's measuring himself against.

**The village economy of a war zone.** Children stealing from the men who came to the village, and acting as brokers for the women — a single, unglamorous paragraph that says more about the war's effect on the surrounding Vietnamese population than almost anything else in the surviving corpus, which otherwise focuses on Charlie's own unit and its patrols.

**No sign-off.** The letter simply stops — "I'll end the letter now & start another one" — without the "Love, Charles" that closes nearly every other letter in the collection. Whatever the second letter was meant to be, if it was written the same day, either it didn't survive or it is the [longer Ripcord letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-08-05-ripcord-and-casualties/) itself.

**Daytona Beach, dreamed of from an actual beach.** A small, wry detail — Charlie pictures the beach he wants to visit with Terrie while writing from a beach he's only passing through on his way back to the jungle.
