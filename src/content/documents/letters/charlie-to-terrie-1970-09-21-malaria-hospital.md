## What the letter is

21 September 1970. Charlie writes Terrie from a Vietnam rear-area hospital on **American National Red Cross stationery** &mdash; the Red Cross cross logo and the printed line *"THE AMERICAN NATIONAL RED CROSS"* across the top of the cream sheet. He has just been admitted with malaria. He is writing rather than waiting in silence because he hasn't had a letter from her since the week before the 17th and he wants to break the gap. The two pages turn from depression to counted blessings &mdash; flush toilets, a real bed, decent food, and someone's tape of Woodstock playing nearby.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**What C Co, 1-506 was doing on 21 September 1970**

Charlie's company was on **Firebase Arsenal**, a mountain firebase in Thua Thien Province (C Co was on Arsenal 12&ndash;29 September). The terrain &mdash; densely forested ridgelines in the western piedmont &mdash; was heavily mosquito-endemic, and malaria was the leading cause of admission to US military hospitals across the 101st AO. Charlie's *"company came in from the field for a week for stand-down and here I sit"* line is structurally accurate: stand-downs concentrated soldiers at rear-area facilities where physical symptoms surfaced after weeks of suppressed presentation in the bush.

Operationally, **Operation Jefferson Glenn had begun on 5 September 1970** &mdash; the last major US ground operation of the Vietnam War, a joint 101st Abn + ARVN 1st Division mission to protect the populated lowlands (the "rocket belt" defense of Hue and Da Nang) and interdict NVA infiltration. 1-506 was a core unit in the rotation. While Charlie was being admitted, his company was about to leave Arsenal on 29 September.

In the medical chain: **the 6th Convalescent Center at Cam Ranh Bay was handling 50&ndash;65% of US Army admissions in 1970 as malaria cases** &mdash; the exact route Charlie would be on within the week. His *"curable / nice receiving kind"* is most likely **vivax malaria**, the strain endemic to I Corps and the one with the better prognosis.

*Sources: [charlie1-506.com firebase rotation table](http://www.charlie1-506.com/html/pages%20from%20main%20nav/when_where.html) (C Co on FB Arsenal 12&ndash;29 Sep 1970); AMEDD Center of History & Heritage, *[Medical Support of the U.S. Army in Vietnam](https://achh.army.mil/history/book-vietnam-medicalsupport-chapter2/)*; [Wikipedia: Operation Jefferson Glenn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Jefferson_Glenn).*

</aside>

The Red Cross provided stationery, postage, and writing-assistance services to hospitalized servicemembers in Vietnam through its field offices at base hospitals across the country. A soldier in a hospital bed with no personal stationery could get a pad of Red Cross paper, a pen, and an envelope to write home. The letterhead on this letter is the direct material trace of that service.

## Transcription

> *Sept 21*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> Guess what! Well, here I am in the hospital suffering from Malaria. It is the curable or "nice" receiving kind. My company came in from the field for a week for stand-down and here I sit while they have it easy too. I should be here a couple more weeks. And then I seems back to those damn mountains. It seems the stupid bastards are really hell-bent on keeping me out there in the rain and all. How am I going to take this year? I really hope I get sick more often. Not malaria, just a broken bone or something.
>
> I'm really depressed. The future just looks mighty bleak. I haven't gotten any letter since the week before the 17th so I guess I will have a lot. Right now it is going to take a lot more than letter to keep me going.
>
> Let me tell you some good things. I'm in unaccustomed comfort. I've seen and used flush toilets and I've been here
>
> [page 2:] the food is good when I can eat and the bed is out of sight. An honest mattress instead of a cot on the ground for a change.
>
> Someone has a tape of Woodstock that I'm listening to. Some is very interesting.
>
> About all I can say now is keep writing and I love you.
>
> Love
> Charles

## What the letter records

**The "curable or 'nice' receiving kind" of malaria.** Vietnam malaria came in two main forms relevant to American troops: **Plasmodium vivax** (treatable, lower mortality, recurring episodes possible) and **Plasmodium falciparum** (more severe, higher mortality, chloroquine-resistant strains common in Vietnam by the late 1960s). The "curable or nice receiving kind" Charlie names is almost certainly **P. vivax** &mdash; the form that responded to standard chloroquine and primaquine treatment without the life-threatening complications of falciparum. The 101st Airborne operating in the wet mountains of I Corps was a high-risk environment for both, but vivax was the more common diagnosis.

**The standdown irony.** Charlie's company had pulled in from the field for the week's stand-down he himself would otherwise have enjoyed; instead he is in the hospital, watching them have an easier week from a bed. The juxtaposition is the central irony of the letter.

**"I really hope I get sick more often."** The line is the unguarded version of the same wish that runs through the corpus: anything to keep him out of the field. Not malaria again, he qualifies, but a broken bone or something less violent than the field. The hospital was the kind of safety the war kept making impossible to find any other way.

**The mail gap.** *"I haven't gotten any letter since the week before the 17th."* A four-or-five-day mail gap from Maryland to a Vietnam APO was normal; what Charlie is describing is roughly that, complicated by the move from the field to the hospital. The letter is his bridge across the silence rather than a reply to anything.

**The inventory of small luxuries.** Flush toilets, an honest mattress, food he can eat &mdash; the second page counts exactly what a cot on the ground and C-rations don't provide. It is the same instinct for concrete detail that runs through every letter, turned here toward comfort instead of danger.

**Someone's tape of Woodstock.** A cassette of the festival (or its soundtrack) circulating through a Vietnam hospital ward in September 1970 is a small, sharp collision of the era's two defining experiences for young American men &mdash; the war and the counterculture moment happening without them at home. Charlie's flat *"some is very interesting"* is characteristically underplayed.

The 28 September letter from Cam Ranh Bay continues the arc.
