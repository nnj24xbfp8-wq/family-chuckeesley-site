## What the letter is

3 October 1970, written over five pages while Charlie convalesced from **malaria** at **Cam Ranh Bay** — the large coastal base far from the mountains where his company was. It is the one letter in the corpus that catches him genuinely *out* of the war for a stretch, and the register is telling: not relief so much as dread of going back. The doctor gave him an extra five days *"because I said I didn't want to go back,"* and Charlie spends the letter loafing, staying dry, and turning over the two long arguments the whole tour runs on — her degree, and when the Army will let him come home. He ends by telling Terrie to *"ignore the offensive parts of this letter,"* his own apology for a teasing passage held private here.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**Malaria, Cam Ranh Bay, and the firebases**

Charlie's note that there was *"no damage... because they now had the medicine to cure Malaria where they didn't back in W.W.II"* reflects the real change: by Vietnam, chloroquine-primaquine treatment and prophylaxis had made malaria survivable and usually non-permanent, where in earlier wars it was often disabling. Malaria was nonetheless one of the largest non-battle casualty causes for U.S. infantry in the I Corps highlands, and evacuation to a rear convalescent facility like **Cam Ranh Bay** was routine.

His geography is precise for the 101st Airborne's late-1970 area of operations: **Firebase O'Reilly** and **Firebase Kathryn** guarded the approaches to the A Sh&#7847;u Valley. By October 1970 the U.S. was handing these positions to the **Army of the Republic of Vietnam (ARVN)** under Vietnamization — exactly what Charlie reports (*"Kathryn is closed down and the S. Vietnamese are the only ones around O'Reilly... doing a good job"*). His flat *"still 70-100 GI's die every week"* is the counterweight to the official drawdown narrative.

*Source: [charlie1-506.com unit history](http://www.charlie1-506.com/) (1-506 Infantry, 101st Airborne Division, I Corps operations, 1970).*

## Transcription

> *Oct 3*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> I'm still at Cam Ranh Bay recovering. Actually I'm all right and when I saw the doctor yesterday he said there was no damage to any of my bodily systems, and that was because they now had the medicine to cure Malaria where they didn't back in W.W.II. Actually I didn't have it that bad. Now I'm just trying to avoid going back to my company. The doctor let me stay here an extra 5 days because I said I didn't want to go back. I'm just loafing around, pulling a few minor details and staying dry. It isn't raining too much here but I know it is where my company is. Although they are in the mountains there seems to be little contact with the enemy anywhere. [Firebase] Kathryn is closed down and the S. Vietnamese are the only
>
> [page 2:] ones around O'Reilly. They seem to be doing a good job and even the action there has slowed down. Still 70-100 GI's die every week so I don't know. Things could still be a lot better here.
>
> I got a lot of letters from you and home today. Yes, you're botching things up by not graduating in June or the summer session. I don't know what I'll do now. I guess I have 250 days to think about it.
>
> *[A short intimate/teasing passage is held private at the family's discretion — the passage Charlie himself flags in his closing line.]*
>
> [page 3:] You mean if I write your parents I can't mention all the money I've given you and I can't slam the military a little? What else is there to talk about?
>
> In all seriousness I do hope you aren't too disappointed and will try your best this year. You ought to be able to do at least as well as a 2.6. I see no sense in your getting a job in Marietta next summer because even if I'm out of the army I probably won't be there much more than two weeks after I get back. Rumors being what they are I don't know exactly when that will be. With the cut back in the army I'm privately hoping for an even bigger drop than 150 days. When I was in California there were some troops coming back with 179 day early outs. I can't really count
>
> [page 4:] on that but it is something for me to dream about. Then I wouldn't even have to extend. First things being first, I just dream of the day I get out of here and the army.
>
> Although I am disappointed you won't graduate in June I still hope you keep going after that damn diploma like there was no tomorrow. Even if you can't teach you could always do something like Vicki. It's just extra influence for you.
>
> I believe I told you papers is out for me as of Oct 1 [and] it was removed from the [R&R] lists in the world. Fair is over. I like you saying though, there's no hurry for china, silver etc. We can still be married next summer, but [it] depends more on me and the service than anything else right now. If they give a large drop from Viet Nam
>
> [page 5:] in order to get the combat troops out, I would be back in the states with time still left to serve. I would hate that too and probably would not want to get married while I was still in the service. I would try for a 90 day early out for school and go to school then though. It all depends how things work out. I'll let you know if & when I hear anything definite.
>
> Right now I have 252 days left in the army and over here. I figure at least 100 of them to be spent in the rear and 150 in the field. God how I dread it. I have only been here 144 days now.
>
> Love
> Charles
>
> *Ignore the offensive parts of this letter*

## What the letter records

**Out of the war, dreading the way back.** *"Now I'm just trying to avoid going back to my company... the doctor let me stay here an extra 5 days because I said I didn't want to go back."* The malaria that put him in Cam Ranh Bay bought him a stretch out of the mountains, and the letter's honest note is how badly he did not want it to end. The dread is quantified at the close — *"at least 100 [days] in the rear and 150 in the field. God how I dread it"* — the same arithmetic of endurance that runs through every field letter.

**"Still 70-100 GI's die every week."** Set flatly against the good news of Vietnamization — Kathryn closed, the ARVN holding O'Reilly, the action slowed — is the number he won't let go unstated. It is the corpus's recurring refusal to let the drawdown story stand alone: the war was winding down and men were still dying weekly.

**The degree, again.** *"Keep going after that damn diploma like there was no tomorrow."* Terrie won't graduate in June, and Charlie — disappointed, but pushing — repeats the education argument he makes from [training](/docs/letters-from-vietnam/) onward. It is the same insistence on schooling that Terrie would carry into raising Chuck, the [family thread](/family/terrie-lee-eesley/) running a generation forward.

**The drop, the marriage, and the order between them.** *"We can still be married next summer, but [it] depends more on me and the service than anything else."* Here the whole engine of the late 1970 letters is stated plainly: an early drop from Vietnam might mean coming home with Army time still to serve, which he'd hate — and might *delay* the marriage rather than hasten it. It's the mirror image of the [28 December letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-12-28-ten-month-rumor-and-married-early/)'s hope to *"get married early,"* the same variables solved a different way.

**"Ignore the offensive parts of this letter."** The closing line is Charlie flagging his own teasing — the passage held private above — and it is characteristic: blunt on the page, then embarrassed by it, apologizing on his way out the door. The same self-editing impulse the [orders-decide-everything letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-training-orders-decide-everything/) names outright (*"I'm not going to edit these and can't"*).
