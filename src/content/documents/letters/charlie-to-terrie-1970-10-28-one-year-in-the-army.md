## What the letter is

28 October 1970, three pages written after four days of steady, chilling rain in the mountains, overdue for resupply. The letter's real weight comes on the second page, where Charlie looks back at his own malaria evacuation with a rare note of second-guessing — *"I do think I came back out too soon"* — before landing on a hard milestone: 9 November will mark a full year wasted in the Army, more than half of it in Vietnam.

## Transcription

> *Oct 28*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> It has been raining steadily for the last 4 days and I'm soaked. It is windy, and although I guess it is around 70-75&deg;, it is still cold and it is very easy to get the chills.
>
> We are still in the down mountains and are supposed to go in for 1 day to dry off as soon as the weather breaks. We are overdue for resupply now, but I still have enough food for 3-4 days so I haven't panicked yet. It is just plain miserable with no relief in sight.
>
> Last time I got mail I got all the letters that had been forwarded to the hospital.
>
> [page 2:] I owe so many people letters but it is almost impossible to write under these conditions.
>
> It sounds like you're doing fine in school, but that is what you said about summer school. Keep studying.
>
> As of now I guess I'll extend to get out of the army, and that (after recalculated) would set me free June 13th, which means 227 days of misery left.
>
> Actually I'm not in too bad shape right now, but I just think this whole mess over here is tiring.
>
> Did I tell you mother made an inquiry through our congressman, and he said he would inquire about me and my situation. I haven't heard anything yet
>
> [page 3:] but I'm just waiting to tell them I'm unfit for the field and how stupid their tactics are. Evidently more than one person thought after I had malaria I would [not] be returned to the field. It never occurred to me there was no way to prevent it anyway, but I do think I came back out too soon.
>
> Oh well!
>
> October is just about over, and Nov 9 I'll have wasted 1 year of my life in the army and over 180 days here in Viet Nam. It goes fast but not fast enough.
>
> No sign of the enemy. I don't know what we would do if we saw him in this weather. We have absolutely no support at this time, it is ludicrous.
>
> In your next package send some sardines, tuna fish, shrimp, and some of the candy/[sandwich] spreads.
>
> Well, don't worry — this weather takes the horniness right out of you. Just sit in the rain and it does it every time.
>
> Love,
> Charles

## What the letter records

**"I do think I came back out too soon."** The rarest kind of admission in the corpus — Charlie second-guessing his own choice rather than the Army's. The [21 September hospital letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-09-21-malaria-hospital/) and its [28 September Cam Ranh Bay follow-up](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-09-28-cam-ranh-bay-convalescence/) both show him consciously stretching his convalescence; here, a month later, back in the mountains and soaked through, he wonders whether he stretched it enough.

**A year wasted, counted to the day.** *"Nov 9 I'll have wasted 1 year of my life in the army."* The anniversary lands exactly where the letter's arithmetic of extension (227 days left if he extends to June 13) makes it feel longest — a full year down, and still more than half his in-country time to go.

**The congressman, still pending.** Picking up from the [24 October letter](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1970-10-24-typhoon-alert-and-165-days/), the congressional inquiry has produced only a promise to look into things. Charlie's plan for what to say if it ever amounts to anything — that he's "unfit for the field" and the tactics are stupid — is as close as he comes in the letters to open institutional criticism.

**No support "at this time."** *"We have absolutely no support at this time, it is ludicrous."* A rare explicit complaint about being under-resourced in the field, paired immediately with the weather joke that closes the letter — Charlie's habitual move from something genuinely bleak to something genuinely funny in the space of two sentences.
