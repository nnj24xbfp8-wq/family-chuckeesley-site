## What the letter is

25 November 1970, the day before Thanksgiving, written from the field in a fine continuous monsoon rain &mdash; and one of the most substantial combat-period letters in the corpus. Charlie has no holiday feeling at all (*"It is really weird"*), reports the small mercy that Pan Am has halved the Hawaii fare, and then does something he rarely does: he answers Terrie's fear about *"contact"* directly, explaining the two recent deaths in the unit in plain, analytic terms. Both, he says, came from things soldiers aren't supposed to do &mdash; and neither is how his own unit operates. It's Charlie reassuring her not with denial but with the grim logic of how men actually died.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**Two deaths, and the leave that wasn't R&R**

Charlie's account &mdash; *"in one the US troops spotted the enemy and chased them at dark and were ambushed and lost 3 men; in the other the element went to sleep on guard"* &mdash; is a rare, sober window into how casualties happened in the day-to-day of an I Corps infantry unit in late 1970, away from set-piece battles: a night pursuit that walked into an ambush, and a lapse on guard. His point to Terrie is reassurance by procedure: *"we don't chase the enemy at night... and very few of us sleep at night."* These are avoidable deaths, and his unit, he's saying, doesn't make those mistakes.

The leave distinction is the other practical thread. **Ordinary leave** ran against a soldier's clock from the moment he left his company until he returned, travel included &mdash; unlike **R&R**, which was a protected stand-down. That's why Charlie, eyeing a possible two-week leave to California rather than the long trip home (he's *"afraid I would go AWOL if I come home"*), keeps weighing it against simply waiting for the Hawaii R&R.

## Transcription

> *Nov 25*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> Well tomorrow is Thanksgiving. I have no sense or feeling that the holidays are upon us. It is really weird. We are in the field once again and it is raining as usual. The heavy monsoons haven't been with us for a while. There is just a misty rain and fog that is fairly continuous. I am able to stay dry though.
>
> Well good news and bad news. The Pan Am airline reduced the 700 to $350 from Calif to there so the total cost to me for transportation would now be only around $550-600. That is still very high. We haven't heard any official word on the leave deal at all. I may go to Calif & stay 2 weeks. Do you want to meet me there. I am really afraid I would go AWOL if I come home. I would be damn stupid to come
>
> [page 2:] back here. Well we must sign up for leave if we want it and they haven't as yet offered it to any of us so I just don't know. Everything is all mixed up.
>
> You are concerned about contact. Well of the two incidents where men were killed: in one the US troops spotted the enemy and chased them at dark and were ambushed and lost 3 men. In the other the element went to sleep on guard. Both things simply aren't supposed to be done. I know we don't chase the enemy at night or even in the day and very few of us sleep at night.
>
> Dear Terrie &mdash; I do love you but I just don't know about coming home for just two weeks &mdash; the leave isn't like R&R as it starts when I leave the company and ends when I get back [&hellip;]
>
> [closing page not present in the surviving scans]

## What the letter records

**Thanksgiving with no Thanksgiving.** The holiday arrives in the field as nothing &mdash; just more misty rain. The flatness is the point: the calendar of home had stopped meaning anything.

**Death explained, not hidden.** Faced with Terrie's worry, Charlie neither minimizes nor dramatizes. He explains exactly how two men died and why it wouldn't happen to him &mdash; the closest the corpus comes to him talking through the actual mechanics of mortal danger, and a measure of how he managed her fear from eight thousand miles away.

**"Afraid I would go AWOL if I come home."** The most honest line in the letter: he doesn't trust himself to come back if he goes all the way home, which is part of why he keeps leaning toward meeting partway, or waiting for R&R. The pull of home is strong enough that he treats it as a risk.
