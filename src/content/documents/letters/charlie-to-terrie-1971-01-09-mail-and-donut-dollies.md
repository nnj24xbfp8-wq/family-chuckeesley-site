## What the letter is

9 January 1971, written on a windy, chilly night while pulling guard. Two of Terrie's letters &mdash; the 27th and the 31st &mdash; have arrived together after the usual mail lag, and they are anxious ones; Charlie answers the anxiety head-on and a little impatiently (*you should have stopped worrying*), then turns to the only forecast he has, which is his own departure. The letter is one more pass at the extension-vs-drop math, and it ends on the Donut Dollies and a line that is half a joke and half a marriage proposal: *I'll be telling you for the rest of your life.*

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**The mail, the math, and the Donut Dollies**

The *"4–5 day mail service"* Charlie complains about was the normal transit for a letter between a I Corps APO and the U.S.; when several of Terrie's letters arrive at once, it is the mail bunching, not her silence, and most of the worry in the corpus traces back to that lag. His departure arithmetic is the same lever pulled all winter: with no drop, he would **extend** his tour so as to discharge directly from Vietnam rather than serve out time stateside &mdash; *"at most now 155 days,"* counting toward a 14 June 1971 ETS.

The **"Donut Dollies"** were the young American women of the Red Cross's **Supplemental Recreational Activities Overseas (SRAO)** program, who ran games, conversation, and "a touch of home" at bases across Vietnam. *Donut Dollies* was the affectionate name used nationwide; the cruder nickname Charlie passes along was barracks slang, and he notes it was never said to their faces. He is narrating the culture to Terrie, not endorsing it &mdash; and immediately reassures her she has no rival in it.

## Transcription

> *Jan 9*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> I got your letters dated 27 and 31 today. Evidently our 4&ndash;5 day mail service isn't working very well. You still seem concerned that I don't love you or something. Well you should have at least received 4&ndash;5 letters before this one so you should have stopped worrying.
>
> It is a windy chilly night and once again I'm writing while on guard. If they keep up the pessimistic rumors you may yet get to go to Hawaii. If there are no drops I think I'll extend to get out of the army over here too. If I extend I'll have at most now 155 days left. If it ever gets warm again it shouldn't be too bad. [&hellip;] Well you are still #1 Terrie.
>
> I hope you don't think I'm comparing you with anyone over here, though. There are some American Red Cross girls over here and are called "Donut Dollies"! Some are pretty sad looking even for [horny] GI's and we fondly call them "Biscuit Bitches," although not to their face. Just thought you would like to know a little of what goes on. I'll be telling you for the rest of your life so don't get too curious.
>
> I'll write again tomorrow night &mdash; esp. if I receive another letter from you.
>
> Love Charles

## What the letter records

**The mail lag as the engine of worry.** *"You should have at least received 4–5 letters before this one."* The single most common misunderstanding in the whole correspondence: Terrie reads a gap in delivery as a gap in feeling, and Charlie, who is writing steadily, keeps having to reassure her across a postal delay neither of them controls.

**"At most now 155 days."** The countdown at its January height &mdash; it would fall through the winter and spring (136, 131, 130) as the drops did their work. The logic is the same one he repeats all season: extend the tour to discharge from Vietnam, and bank every administrative advantage toward an earlier real exit.

**"I'll be telling you for the rest of your life so don't get too curious."** The tenderest line in the January letters, and the truest to where they were heading. He is narrating a whole world &mdash; the base, the Donut Dollies, the war &mdash; to the person he means to spend that life telling it to. Seven months later they were married.
