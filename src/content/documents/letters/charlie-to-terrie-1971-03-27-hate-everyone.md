## What the letter is

27 March 1971. Two days after the steady, planning-minded 25 March letter, this one drops straight through the floor. It is short, and for Charlie unusually unguarded: a mail gap, the old question about Terrie's parents, and then the plain statement of a man worn thin &mdash; *I really need someone to talk to. I'm beginning to hate everyone around me. These people are really poor excuses. This place is really getting to me.* No firebase, no enemy, no incoming. The thing wearing on him is the rear: the brass and lifers of the office, the absence of anyone he can actually talk to, and the slow grind of waiting out a war from a desk. He catches himself, ends almost mid-thought &mdash; *well I guess I'll read a book* &mdash; and signs it *I love you.*

**The rear-area low, late March 1971**

This is the shadow side of the safety Charlie was so grateful for two days earlier. Pulled off the line into a rear job at the Quang Tri / Da Nang support area during Lam Son 719, he was out of physical danger and into a different kind of attrition: long hours, bad mail, career soldiers he despised, and no one he could confide in. The corpus reads, across these weeks, as a steady alternation between relief at being out of the field and a flat, isolating boredom &mdash; and 27 March is the day the second feeling wins. It is worth reading directly against the 25 March letter (*"I need nothing now except for time to pass"*) and the lighter 18 March one (*"a good feeling getting better"*): the same man, the same tour, swinging week to week as the count ran down.

## Transcription

> *March 27*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> I haven't heard from you for a while. I assume everything is all right. Have you asked your parents yet. I hope you don't fight them but that they let you go. I really need someone to talk to. I'm beginning to hate everyone around me. These people are really poor excuses. This place is really getting to me. Well I guess I'll read a book. I love you.
>
> Love, Charles

## What the letter records

**The mail gap as mood.** Across the whole correspondence, a stretch with no letter from Terrie reliably precedes Charlie's lowest entries. Here he names it in the first line and then, rather than performing cheerfulness, simply lets the isolation show.

**"These people are really poor excuses."** His contempt for the rear-echelon world &mdash; the "brass and lifers" of the 5 March letter &mdash; curdles here into something closer to despair. The relief of being off the line came with a cost he does not usually admit: he had nobody around him he wanted to be around.

**"Well I guess I'll read a book."** The deflection that closes the letter is characteristic. Having said the hard thing, he steps back from it, names a small ordinary act to fill the evening, and reaches for Terrie in three words before signing off.
