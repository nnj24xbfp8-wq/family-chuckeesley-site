## What the letter is

18 December 1970. Charlie opens by telling Terrie he's sober now and will try to write a more pleasant letter than the last one &mdash; a small window onto how much of the late-1970 correspondence was written drinking. The body is the usual late-tour limbo: a 50-50 chance of Hawaii depending on the drop, a few quiet lines of personal planning kept between them, the apology for being behind on letters. And then, dropped in almost casually, the single hardest thing he ever reports doing &mdash; or refusing to do: he was nearly sent back to the field for not firing at children to scare them off the wire.

<aside class="not-prose my-8 border-l-4 border-amber-700 bg-amber-50/60 dark:bg-amber-950/20 px-6 py-5 rounded-r">

**The children at the fence**

*"Would you believe that I almost got sent back to the field because I didn't shoot at children to scare them away from a fence near my guard post?"* This is the moral center of the December letters. Vietnamese children worked the perimeters of U.S. bases constantly &mdash; begging, selling, scavenging, trading through the wire (the same kids Charlie describes elsewhere [selling drugs and cigarettes through the fence](/docs/letters/charlie-to-terrie-1971-01-09-mail-and-donut-dollies/)). Standing orders at guard posts often called for warning shots to drive them back, both as security and to deter the use of children to probe defenses. Charlie wouldn't fire near them, and his unit treated that as a punishable failure &mdash; hence his flash of hatred for the *"sorry lifers."* It is the clearest instance in the corpus of his quiet refusal to do the war's casual cruelties, and of what that refusal cost him.

*Source: context on perimeter security and civilian children at U.S. bases, [Wikipedia: Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War).*

</aside>

## Transcription

> *Dec 18*
>
> Dear Terrie,
>
> I got your letter from Dec 12-13 today. I'm sober now so I guess I'll see if I can write a more pleasant letter today. Yes boom-boom is [&hellip;] but I'm still waiting for you. At least it won't cost anything. There is a 50-50 chance of Hawaii. It all depends whether I get a "drop" from here (the 10-month tour). I may see you March or April at the latest, either there or in Hawaii. I'll be able to give you a month['s] warning as that is all I'll have.
>
> [&hellip; a few lines on family planning are kept private at the family's discretion &hellip;]
>
> I'll send this letter to Thomas. I'm way behind on my letter writing so I hope somehow to catch up in the next few days.
>
> Would you believe that I almost got sent back to the field because I didn't shoot at children to scare them away from a fence near my guard post? Well if it comes right down to it I'll be going back to the field. I really hate no end the sorry "lifers" for a day or two.
>
> Well [Merry X-mas] and my next letter will be to Maryland.
>
> Love Charles

## What the letter records

**"I'm sober now."** The opening is a quiet admission about the texture of the season's letters &mdash; several were written drinking, and this one is an apology-by-tone for an earlier one. The December rear-area lull, the guard shifts, the boredom and the booze all sit behind it.

**The 50-50 Hawaii odds.** The whole spring's Hawaii-or-home question is here in embryo, hinging entirely on the *drop*: if the Army cut his tour, he'd go straight home and skip R&R; if not, he'd take R&R in Hawaii. He can give Terrie only a month's warning either way.

**"My next letter will be to Maryland."** A small but useful marker: Terrie was moving from Marietta to the University of Maryland for the spring 1971 semester, and the letters' mailing address shifts accordingly right here.
